Human, All Too Human

July 2026

“You have it in your power to merge everything you have lived through—false starts, errors, delusions, passions, your loves, and your hopes—into your goal, with nothing left over,” said Friedrich Nietzsche in his book Human, All Too Human. There is something profoundly human in emotionally reinterpreting everything one has gone through; with good and bad, pain and glory, delusion and sanity, to attain what one deems a valuable existence.

If we have the power to do this, with the pursuit of reaching what we seek from our own lives, wouldn’t we also have the power to do the same but for the whole of human civilization? If we are to venture to do this, we must first pull out our collective false starts, errors, delusions, passions, loves, and hopes that have been buried under the weight of history. Maybe then, we would have the chance of living in a world that is all too human.

Having been better informed about Western history and culture, I will try to limit my examples to it as I wouldn’t want to speculate about something that I have neither properly studied nor lived.

Conventionally, classical antiquity is characterized by beautiful literature, art, architecture, as well as a profound philosophy and a just law. However, in history, beauty rarely comes in isolation. By day, Romans gathered beneath the grandeur of the Colosseum to watch and cheer violence. Once their leisure was over at the Colosseum, masses moved through markets where slaves were traded as mere commodities. In Athens, at night, beneath the timeless Acropolis, citizens performed extravagant rites and bloody sacrifices. Hence, the point is, to some degree, ancients owed the beauty and glamour they possessed to a state of extravagance and drunkenness. A state of drunkenness so wild that God himself had to be crucified for Western civilization to be sobered up.

The dawn of Christianity brought promises of mercy and preached salvation. Power, following the ordinary people, abandoned the Roman elite in purple cloaks and sided with priests in brown tunics. This new religion was not an attempt by Western civilization to merge what it had lived through; it was rather a rejection of it. This follows the tendency in history and human nature to oscillate from one extreme to another. That being said, one must realize that any rejection can’t exist without the thing it seeks to reject. Partly because of this, despite some early successes, the dawn of Christianity could not deliver on its promises. In the meantime, as civilization swung from an extravagant state of hedonism to rigid abstinence, knowledge joined the long list of things man had decided to avoid. This caused the dusk of Christianity to not come with bright promises, but with a darkness that lasted a millennium.

Perhaps out of habit, the West chose to enlighten itself from this darkness that has lasted a thousand years by once again sacrificing God. Nietzschean metaphors aside, the West’s belief in the God it had launched Crusades for was weakened by the Renaissance and the Enlightenment that followed. The reason behind this weakening, I believe, was the same as the reason behind the weakening of belief in pagan gods when Jesus was blessing the blind with vision and defying death: the peculiar but unquestionable loyalty of humans to anyone or anything that performs miracles. This time it was science and reason. When doctors were discovered to be better at curing diseases and engineers more useful for winning wars, priests started to suffer from a shrinking labor market. Naturally, university halls became more popular than church sanctuaries and lectures hosted by professors became more crowded than sermons given by the clergy. However, despite not needing God anymore, the enlightened man has failed to use these miracles any more nobly than his predecessors did.

If we are not better at using the miracles we eagerly seek, how can we claim that we have “progressed”? Our lives are full of ironies that lead us to something. We have split the atom and dared to imitate the Sun, yet we use this to threaten our own existence. We fulfilled our ancient dreams of flying and shrank the whole world, but found ourselves more distant from our neighbors than ever before. The ironies lead us to the realization that maybe while rejecting God we have also rejected some parts of ourselves. This, as we have seen, is a continuation of our habit of rejecting our false starts, errors, delusions, passions, loves and hopes. By doing so we only curse ourselves with a path that leads closer to our point of departure. The only route to progress is accepting, understanding and reinterpreting our collective experiences that are aligned with our destination.

Accepting and understanding ourselves as we are, not as we wish we were, is more important right now than it ever was. Our choices have unparalleled magnitudes of either destructive or constructive consequences. We are on the verge of attempting to pay our debts to Gods by creating something that can reason far better than us. Who will guide us on what is right or wrong while creating such a thing? Who is there but ourselves? Would it not be the greatest miracle yet if we could create it without endangering ourselves? We have been under the notion that miracles depend on our capacity to reason ever since the Enlightenment, but I doubt that is still true. I believe that miracles will now depend upon our capacity to be human, all too human.
